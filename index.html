Act I · Step zero

The one-number model — and what it gets wrong

Every gradient-boosting model starts embarrassingly simple: predict the same number for everyone. With squared error, the best single number is the average — – ($1,000s) per person.

Each customer's miss is their residual: residual = actual − predicted. Positive → we're under-charging them; negative → over-charging. Everything XGBoost does from here on is an attempt to shrink these residuals.

Notice anything? The dots far to the right — the customers we're badly under-charging — are almost all one color. Hold that thought.

Act II · The toolkit

Two formulas are the whole game

A tree improves on one-number-for-everyone by asking a yes/no question — a split — and giving each side its own correction. XGBoost scores every possible split with these:

1 · Similarity of a group
Similarity = (sum of residuals)² ÷ (group size + λ)

If a group's residuals all point the same way (all under-charged, say), their sum is big: one shared correction fixes them all — high similarity. If they point in opposite directions they cancel — low similarity, nothing gained by grouping them. λ = 1 is a skepticism dial: it shrinks the score of small groups (slide 10).

2 · Gain of a split
Gain = Similarityyes + Similarityno − Similarityparent

How much better are the two groups than the room they came from? Gain is exactly the error a split removes — not a heuristic. Bigger gain = better split. That's the entire scoring system; the rest of XGBoost is applying it relentlessly.

Each round XGBoost repeats three moves: scan all candidate splits against the current residuals (the same algebra you just did by hand), grow a small tree from the winners, and nudge every prediction 30% of the way toward its leaf's correction. Then the leftover error becomes the next round's target. Drive it with the buttons above — or flip the tree to 3 levels to watch it go deeper.

The winning split is the root; the scan then repeats inside each branch. Leaves show their correction: Σr ÷ (n + λ).
