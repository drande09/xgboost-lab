Step zero: the one-number model

Every gradient-boosting model starts embarrassingly simple: predict the same number for everyone. With squared error, the best single number is the average — here – ($1,000s) per person.

For each customer, the amount that prediction misses by is their residual: residual = actual − predicted. A positive residual means we're under-charging them; negative means over-charging. Everything XGBoost does from here on is an attempt to shrink these residuals.

Part 02 · You be the algorithm

Split the room. We'll run the numbers.

A tree improves on the one-number model by asking a yes/no question — a split — and giving each side its own prediction. XGBoost scores every possible split with two pieces of algebra, then keeps the best. Your turn first.

The two formulas you need
Similarity of a group  =  (sum of its residuals)² ÷ (group size + λ)
Gain of a split  =  Similarityleft + Similarityright − Similarityparent

Intuition: if a group's residuals all point the same way (all under-charged, say), their sum is big and the group is easy to fix with one shared correction — high similarity. If they point in opposite directions they cancel out — low similarity, nothing gained by grouping them. λ (lambda) = 1 here; it's a skepticism dial we'll return to in Part 04. Bigger gain = better split.

Part 03 · XGBoost's turn

No guessing. Every split, every round.

Here's the actual algorithm, with nothing hidden: each round it (1) scans every candidate split and keeps the winner, (2) grows a small tree (two levels deep here), and (3) nudges every prediction a fraction of the way toward what the tree suggests. Then it recomputes residuals and goes again — each new tree fixing what the last ones missed. Drive it yourself with the buttons.
